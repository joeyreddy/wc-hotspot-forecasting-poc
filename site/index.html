Synthetic data. Every incident location and time on this page is invented. The number of crimes per police station per month is real (SAPS, April 2021 to September 2026), so totals, trends and the stations that matter are genuine; where inside a precinct, and when, each crime happened is simulated.

Hotspot forecasting for the Western Cape: proof of concept

What the province could do with incident-level crime data. At the end of each month the system ranks every 500 m block in the populated Western Cape (19,848 blocks across 152 police precincts) and flags the 5% where crime is most likely next month. Below, five forecasting methods are tested on 24 months they had not seen, using the standard measures crime analysts use.

How the methods compare

Hit rate: share of next month's crimes inside the flagged 5%. PAI (Prediction Accuracy Index): hit rate divided by area share; 1 is no better than random. PEI: crimes caught as a share of the most that any choice of the same number of blocks could have caught. Averages over 24 test months, October 2024 to September 2026.

Hit rate by month

Do crimes cluster in space and time?

Knox test on murders and attempted murders, last 12 months: how many more pairs of crimes occur close together in both space and time than if timing were random. Above 1 means near-repeat clustering, the pattern self-exciting models exploit.

On real data this test answers whether retaliation and contagion effects exist in Western Cape gang violence. Here they were built into the simulation, so the result shows the method works, not that the effect is real.

How reliable are the ranges?

Share of police stations whose actual monthly count fell inside the forecast's 80% range. A plain Poisson model assumes too little variation; a negative-binomial model, which allows extra variation, gets closer to 80%.

Who gets flagged?

Flagging follows where crime is reported. A real deployment would also check flagged areas by income and race, and track whether patrols shift crime elsewhere.

The feedback-loop trap

Simulation of police-detected crime such as drugs: detections depend on where police already patrol. Sending patrols wherever detections were highest locks onto the starting areas. The detections look more and more concentrated, which reads as success, while patrols drift away from where activity really is. Planning on crime the public reports avoids the trap.

From proof of concept to a working system

What this proves, and what it does not

  • Proves: the full pipeline runs end to end at provincial scale. That covers building a 500 m grid with population, placing incidents, forecasting every block monthly, scoring with standard measures, checking uncertainty, auditing who is flagged and simulating feedback loops.
  • Proves: the evaluation can tell methods apart. Simple "last month" hotspots do worst; methods that combine long-run hotspots with recent nearby crime do best.
  • Does not prove: that these accuracy levels hold for the Western Cape. Locations, timing and clustering are simulated, and the real numbers can only come from real incident data.
  • Safeguards: area-level only, no personal data, police-detected crime kept separate, ranges shown with every forecast, and human decisions on deployment.

Method: monthly SAPS station totals for murder, attempted murder, robbery with aggravating circumstances and residential burglary (152 Western Cape precincts) are spread over 500 m blocks in proportion to population, persistent neighbourhood risk and recent nearby crime. Models are refitted on data before each test month. Code: poc/ in the CrimeStats repository.
